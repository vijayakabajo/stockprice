What is a SPAC?

A SPAC, or special purpose acquisition company, is a shell company that sells shares in an IPO to raise cash, then uses that cash to merge with a private operating business.

SPACs are also called blank check companies, because investors fund them before anyone knows which business they will buy. The SEC's investor bulletin on SPACs covers both stages: the shell before a deal, and the merged company after it [1].

How does a SPAC work?

A SPAC raises money, holds it in a trust account, searches for a private company to merge with, and either completes that merger or returns the money by a deadline.

  1. IPO. The SPAC lists on an exchange and sells shares, often bundled with warrants.
  2. Trust account. The IPO money is held by a third party until a merger closes or the SPAC liquidates [1].
  3. Search. The sponsors look for a private company to acquire, within a deadline set in the SPAC's charter.
  4. Vote and redemption. Shareholders vote on the proposed deal and can usually redeem their shares for cash from the trust instead.
  5. De-SPAC. The merger closes, the private company becomes the listed company, and the ticker usually changes.

Why do SPAC profiles on this site look thin?

A SPAC has no operating business before its merger, so it has no revenue, products or meaningful headcount to report.

SPAC profiles show what does exist, such as the listing, identifiers, market cap and filings, and are kept out of search engine indexes until a merger turns the shell into an operating company.

What should investors check before buying a SPAC?

The SEC suggests reviewing the sponsors' backgrounds and the SPAC's IPO prospectus and periodic reports on EDGAR, because a SPAC has no operating history to judge [1].

Those filings also set out the sponsors' economic interests, the warrant terms and the merger deadline [2]. Warrants that are not exercised in time can expire worthless.
