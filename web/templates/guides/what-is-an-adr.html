What is an ADR?

An American depositary receipt (ADR) is a certificate issued by a U.S. depositary bank that represents shares of a non-U.S. company and trades in U.S. dollars.

How do ADRs work?

A U.S. bank holds shares of the foreign company and issues ADRs against them, so each ADR represents one share, a fraction of a share or several shares.

That ratio lets an ADR trade at a price typical of U.S. shares. ADRs trade and settle in U.S. dollars through U.S. systems, so holders avoid dealing in a foreign currency, and they can usually be exchanged back for the underlying shares [1].

How long have ADRs existed, and how many are there?

The first ADR was created in 1927, and in 2012 the SEC counted more than 2,000 ADRs representing companies from more than 70 countries [1].

How is an ADR different from U.S. common stock?

An ADR adds a depositary bank between the investor and the company, which brings currency, fee and reporting differences.

  • Currency. The ADR is priced in dollars, but the business earns in its home currency, so exchange rates affect its value.
  • Fees. Depositary banks may charge custody fees, often deducted from dividends.
  • Reporting. Many foreign issuers file an annual Form 20-F instead of a 10-K, on a different schedule [2].
  • Voting. Shareholder votes usually pass through the depositary bank.
